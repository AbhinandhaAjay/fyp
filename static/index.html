How rain forms. Water is always on the move. Heat from the sun warms oceans, lakes and rivers, and some of that water turns into vapour, an invisible gas that rises into the air. This step is called evaporation.

Plants add to it too. They pull water up from the soil through their roots and release vapour through tiny openings in their leaves. This is called transpiration, and a single large tree can release hundreds of litres on a hot day.

As warm, moist air rises it cools. Cool air cannot hold as much vapour, so the vapour condenses onto tiny specks of dust, salt or smoke, forming droplets. Billions of these droplets together make a cloud.

Inside a cloud, droplets bump into each other and join. When they grow too heavy for the rising air to hold them up, they fall as precipitation: rain, or snow and hail when it is cold enough.

Rain that reaches the ground either soaks into the soil, becoming groundwater, or flows over the surface into streams and rivers. Eventually most of it returns to the sea, where the sun warms it again.

Because the same water keeps cycling, the water you drink today may once have fallen as rain thousands of years ago. The total amount of water on Earth stays almost the same; it only changes form and place.
